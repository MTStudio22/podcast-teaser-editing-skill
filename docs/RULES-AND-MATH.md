# Podcast Teaser Editing — Rules, Math, and Proof

Research notes on long-form podcast trailer/teaser editing, compiled from Editing Explained's "Podcast Trailer Editing" masterclass, then cross-checked against two real-world trailers played and dissected live in that same class.

## The 3 foundational rules

1. **Cut for the avatar, not the ego.** Every clip choice is filtered through the target viewer's profile, never the editor's personal taste. Profile your avatar in 5 questions before touching a single clip: who are they, what do they do, their income/budget level, what they're here to learn/get, and what stage of that journey they're at.
2. **Context over clutter.** Fewer, well-chosen clips that build real context beat a pile of "this looked interesting" cuts. Every numeric cap below exists to enforce this rule — when unsure whether to keep a clip, the test is whether cutting it costs context, or whether it's clutter wearing a different marker's label.
3. **Provoke emotion.** The cut should land an emotional beat for that specific avatar — a relatable pain point, a rhythm break (sudden silence), a bold claim — not just be "interesting."

## The math behind each marker

| Marker | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| **Time** | 1hr–1h45m source podcast → trailer length 1:10–1:30. 2hr+ source → only 50s–1:10 — shorter, not longer, because viewers who click a 2hr+ video are already high-intent and don't need convincing. No validated numbers exist for sources under 1hr or in the 1h45m–2hr gap — treat extrapolations there as unproven. |
| **Music** | Cinematic violin is the best-validated default instrument across the source creator's viral videos. BPM: 100 when the speaker's energy is already high; 150–190 to enhance when energy is lower. Switch instrument/BPM per segment as context/energy shifts. Test your own variations rather than copying blindly. |
| **Pattern interrupts** | Minimum 1, max 2 for sustained audience quality (some podcasts run higher, not recommended for retention). 1 interrupt → place at 45–55% of trailer length. 2 interrupts → first at ~30%, second at ~80%. |
| **Questions** | Cap at ~30% of total statement weight. A question that resolves into a statement mid-sentence doesn't count toward the ratio. |
| **Bold statements** | Under 1 min trailer → 7–9 statements. 1–1:40 trailer → 10–13 max, never more (going past this breaks Rule 2). |
| **Ending bridge** | The final ~15 seconds matter as much as the first 15. Must land exactly ONE (never mixed) of: (a) **status upgrade / ego hit** — elevate or challenge the viewer's identity; (b) **perceived value** — imply outsized value just given ("I should've charged $500 for this"); (c) **authority with proof** — a credibility claim backed by evidence, not a bare assertion. |

## How the markers actually interact (synthesis)

This part isn't stated outright in the source material — it's what falls out when the markers are cross-referenced against each other and against the real trailer breakdowns below.

- **Avatar is upstream of every other marker.** Rule 1 should also decide which of the 3 ending-bridge types to use: a skeptical/price-sensitive avatar needs authority-with-proof to trust the claim; an avatar already sold on the topic responds to status-upgrade; a curious-but-undecided avatar responds to perceived value.
- **Markers stack on the same clips rather than each getting a separate slot.** In both real breakdowns below, a single clip does double or triple duty — a line can be a bold statement AND a pattern interrupt at once, or perceived value riding on an already-counted bold statement. The practical implication: don't budget pattern interrupts/ending bridge as *additions* to the statement count — look for beats that can serve two markers at once.
- **Time and statement density trade off inversely.** A 2hr+ podcast gets a *shorter* trailer than a 1–1:45hr one, with no signal the statement cap drops to match — so the surviving cut is denser per second the longer the source runs.
- **Music and pattern interrupts should move together.** A pattern interrupt is a rhythm break by definition — exactly the moment a BPM/instrument switch reads as intentional instead of random.

## The live editing workflow

Demonstrated live in DaVinci Resolve, not written on any slide:

1. Build a timeline, drop the full raw podcast on it.
2. Watch the entire raw podcast once, calmly, start to finish — no scrubbing, no fast-forward.
3. Mark every candidate moment with color-coded markers as you go: green = certain fit (avatar + a rule, will use), orange = backup/maybe (use only if the statement quota isn't otherwise met), red = questions (tracked separately against the 30% cap).
4. For each marked clip, check whether it can serve 2+ markers at once — those are the highest-priority picks.
5. Duplicate the timeline ("stacked timelines") so the original marked timeline stays untouched as reference; never cut directly into it.
6. On the new timeline, pull marked clips up and arrange for pacing.
7. Place pattern interrupt(s) per the ratio above.
8. Lay in music per the BPM lane decided from speaker energy; switch tracks where energy/context shifts.
9. Build the ending bridge in the final ~15 seconds using the single bridge type chosen for this avatar.
10. Confirm the final 15 seconds got as much attention as the first 15 (the opening hook).

## Proof: validated against real trailers

The numbers above aren't invented — the source creator describes extracting them from hundreds of hours across his own channel's top-performing long-form videos (manually comparing top vs. bottom performers), then validating live against two real trailers:

- **External validation #1 — a 15M-subscriber interview podcast's actual trailer** (a Robert Greene episode): played live, shown landing at ~1:30 — matching the 1hr–1h45m time band exactly — and individually dissected: a line combining a bold statement with a pattern interrupt, and a 20-second sustained statement used as a live example of context-over-clutter.
- **External validation #2 — the source creator's own channel trailer** (a film emulation masterclass episode): dissected the same way — the question cap held even though the trailer felt question-heavy on a first watch, and both the pattern interrupt and ending bridge were identified live in real time.

One gap: no explicit numbers were given for podcasts under 1 hour, or in the 1h45m–2hr range. Treat any numbers used in those bands as extrapolated, not proven, until tested against your own data.
